Confident and Wrong

A short, true story about setting up this hackathon's infrastructure — in which almost everything researched carefully turned out to need checking, and three things were simply false.

Nothing here was a hard technical problem. Every failure was the same shape: something looked settled, nobody had run it, and running it took ten minutes.

One

What was asked for

The organisers needed a set of cloud tools that 25 student teams could use for 48 hours, with a fixed budget and no software installed on anyone's laptop. Six services had been proposed by a group of experienced engineers: a browser code editor, a gateway for AI models, a starter repository, a database, a sandbox, and somewhere to host the result.

The request to Claude was simple: check this list is right, and make it cheap.

Two

What it proposed

Claude read the official pricing and documentation pages for about thirty services and came back with a confident answer. Keep two of the six. Replace two. Drop two. Add one nobody had suggested. It set a budget of $12 per team, and it named the exact AI models each team should use, chosen from a price table.

It was a good brief. It was well argued, it cited its sources, and it was mostly right.

But every single word of it came from reading, not from running. No key had been created. No model had been called. No test had been run. The brief described what companies say their products do.

Three

The four things nobody could confirm

Claude listed four claims it could not verify from any official page. One of them mattered far more than the rest:

“If you set a spending limit on a team's API key, that team cannot overspend.”

The whole budget rested on this. The documentation described the setting and showed you how to read the remaining balance — but it never actually said what happens when a team hits the limit. Does it stop them? Or does it just keep a tally while the money drains away?

Nobody knew. So we tested it. Cost: ten cents.

Four

Testing the spending limit took four attempts

Three of them failed. And here is the part worth sitting with: all three failed because of bugs in the test, not in the thing being tested.

TRY 1

Optimised for the wrong thing

The script picked the cheapest model available, out of habit. But the goal was to spend ten cents as fast as possible. At that price it needed roughly 800 calls — over half an hour of waiting. Killed it.

TRY 2

Declared victory on the wrong error

It picked an expensive model, got an error back, and printed HARD BLOCK CONFIRMED. It was wrong. The error meant “this model can't be called this way”, not “you're out of money”. The script treated any failure as proof of the failure it was hoping for. The tell was in its own output: money spent, $0.00.

TRY 3

Used the wrong key

The fix used an admin key to call a model. Admin keys can't call models — a fact written in that script's own opening comment, by the same author, an hour earlier.

TRY 4

Worked — and the answer was better than hoped

The limit doesn't just stop a team when they run out. It refuses a request before running it if the worst case would cost more than they have left. The budget is a genuine ceiling, not an estimate. Three cents to know that for certain.

Five

Then the model choice fell over

Claude had already picked which AI model every team would use, reasoning from a price table: cheapest of the capable ones, biggest memory. Sensible. It was written into the configuration file.

Before shipping it, we wrote a twenty-line test. Give six models the same badly-written business idea. Each must reply with a structured verdict — a decision plus a list of specific problems. Score four things: does the reply parse, does it have the right shape, does it correctly reject a bad idea, and are its complaints specific rather than vague.

The chosen model scored zero out of three. It could not produce a usable answer at all.

Had that shipped, 25 teams would have opened the starter kit, watched the core loop fail on every attempt, and reasonably concluded the architecture was broken. It would have cost the event a day. The test that caught it cost about four cents.

Six

And then we ran the same test again

Three tries per model felt like enough. It wasn't. We reran it with five, and the picture changed.

The middle row is the dangerous one. A model that fails every time is annoying but honest — you find out immediately. A model that works three times and then doesn't is far worse, because the failure looks like your bug. You will spend an hour at 3am rewriting a prompt that was never the problem.

Three tries wasn't evidence. It was an anecdote with a table around it.

Three separate bugs in the tests. Wrong optimisation, wrong error interpreted as success, wrong credential.

Eight

What to take from this

A confident answer is not a verified answer

This applies to documentation, which describes intent rather than behaviour. It applies to an AI assistant, which will give you a well-reasoned, well-cited recommendation with no way of knowing it is wrong. And it applies to you. The fluency of an answer tells you nothing about whether it is true — and fluent wrong answers are the expensive kind, because they don't feel like they need checking.

Your test can be wrong too — and it will fail silently

Three of the four failures here were in the test, not the system. The worst one printed CONFIRMED in capital letters while measuring nothing at all. Before you trust a result, ask the harder question: could this test tell the difference between the outcomes I care about? A check that can't distinguish “out of money” from “wrong endpoint” will confidently report whichever one you were hoping for.

Run it twice before you believe it

Language models are not deterministic, even with randomness disabled. One clean run is an anecdote. Two runs found a model that complies 40% of the time — the exact failure that would have eaten someone's night, because it looks like a bug in your own code.

None of this required skill. It required ten minutes and a willingness to check something that already felt settled. That is the whole discipline.

Over the 48 hours you'll make dozens of assumptions of exactly this kind — about a model, a library, a rate limit, your own code. You cannot test all of them. So test the ones your project would collapse without, and write those tests so they can tell you that you were wrong.
